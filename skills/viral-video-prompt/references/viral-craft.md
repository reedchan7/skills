# Viral craft — hooks, rhythm, DNA, and category playbooks

Phase 2 in full, plus the reference material Phase 3 draws on. Facts read
2026-09-04 from TikTok's Creative Center, Creative Codes, and TikTok Shop
Academy, from named creative datasets, and from Chinese platform practitioners.
Evidence grades: **A** platform-published or peer-reviewed · **B** a named
dataset with published method · **C** practitioner convention.

## The finding that shapes everything

The largest creative dataset here, covering $1.29B of paid spend, defines a
winner as a creative taking at least ten times the account's median spend, and
finds the hit rate is about **five percent**. Its own reading: low hit rates are
a statistical feature, not a quality signal, and volume creates more chances to
surface a winner without changing the per-creative odds. [A]

So **viral DNA is a compression of what to try, not a formula that raises the
odds of any single clip.** The lever is structural diversity across a batch:
different hook family, different format, different proof beat. Two prompts that
share a mechanism are one prompt. That is why this skill ships two rival bets
rather than one optimised prompt.

## The hook

**The window.** TikTok's own numbers put the decision in the first two seconds
for ad recall and two and a half for awareness; ninety percent of ad-recall
impact is captured within six seconds. [A] Chinese practice calls the same
window 黄金3秒.

**The rule that matters most for e-commerce**: the hook must be *related to the
product*. TikTok Shop's own guidance says a product-related opening is what lets
the hook flow into the message; an entertaining but disconnected opener creates
a discontinuity that undermines the commercial purpose. [A] A generic pattern
interrupt with nothing to do with the bag is deprecated by the platform that
distributes it.

**Front-load the text.** A call to action in the opening frame lifts conversion
by 44% against showing it later; a title at the start lifts brand recall 48%.
On-screen text within the first seven seconds lifted conversion 43% in TikTok's
e-commerce data. [A]

**Three layers inside three seconds** [C, but each layer is separately
evidenced]: a visual trigger at 0.0–0.5s, the spoken or written promise landing
by 1.5s, and the context locked by 3.0s.

### Openings that are not hooks

These read as hooks and are not, because nothing happens and nothing is at
stake. Each is a common failure of AI-generated product video specifically,
since the models render them beautifully. [C]

- A city aerial, an architectural pan, or a sunrise held for two or three
  seconds.
- A person slowly walking in, sitting down, arriving home, or waking up.
- The product emerging slowly from darkness with no event.
- A single product held or floating centre-frame for a beat before it glows,
  opens, or breaks apart.
- An isolated visual anomaly with no who, where, or why.
- Cinematic lighting plus particles plus smoke plus a slow push.
- A logo or slogan first, with no visual problem behind it.
- Unrelated curiosity bait that the clip explains later.

### Openings that are almost hooks

These are harder, because they look like work and they pass a careless review.
Each is a competent shot with no tension in it, and each has the same cure:
**put the claim in the image, and make the product look inadequate first.**

| The near-miss | Why it fails | The cure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A hand lowers an object into a container, top-down, on a wooden surface | the category's stock advertisement; nothing is at stake, because the object obviously fits | show the container looking too small beside the object first, or show the object failing to fit something else |
| A card carrying the tension over a picture that has none | the words work and the image is wallpaper, so the clip dies the moment the reader has read the card | the card should *confirm* what the frame already proposes, never supply it |
| A before-and-after whose frame one holds only the "before" | the mechanism works because both states are visible at once; a payoff at second nine is not a hook | put both states in frame one — split frame, or the finished state already sitting behind the mess. Then check the row below, because this cure has a failure of its own |
| Both states in frame one, arranged as a top-down flat lay | the argument is real and nobody perceives it: an inventory has to be *scanned*, and a scrolling viewer does not scan. It reads as a catalogue photograph | keep the two states, drop the arrangement. Reduce the claim to one comparison between two objects that touch or share a baseline, so it is taken in rather than worked out |
| The product alone, beautifully lit, doing something small | this is a packshot with motion painted on | give the frame a second object that makes a comparison possible |
| A hook whose subject is the problem rather than the product | the platform's own guidance requires a product-related opening, and the mess is not the product | the product is in frame one and visibly implicated in the problem |

## Rhythm and retention

Platforms rank on watch time and completion first, then saves and shares, then
likes; replays sit in the strongest tier. [A] Do not encode a completion
threshold: the published benchmarks disagree because they measure different
things. At five to fifteen seconds the leverage sits almost entirely in the
first three seconds and in whether the ending invites a replay.

**Scene variety is measured.** Videos showing a variety of scenes converted 38%
better than one person selling in a continuous shot; transitions lift brand
recall 53%. [A] Something on screen should change every **two to three
seconds**, and plain jump cuts alone are worth 10–15 percentage points of
average watch time against a static shot. [B] TikTok's own shoppable-video
guidance illustrates the standard with a bag shown from eleven angles in one
clip. [A]

**Budget changes, not cuts** — `references/viral-anatomy.md` §3 has the full
argument and the table of what renders. Short version: these families do not cut
on request, so a rhythm built on cuts arrives as a single take or a torn frame.
Build it from changes the models do render — an object entering, a hand arriving,
a state flip, a scale jump, a card, a camera move starting. **Five seconds wants
three changes, ten seconds five, fifteen seconds seven to nine**, of which one or
two may be cuts. No single state holds longer than three seconds, the hook
included, and the first change lands by 1.5 s.

**Three ideas, not six.** The average ad in a classified sample carries 2.7
content blocks. [B] A dense change map is not a licence for more claims: one
proof is the floor and three selling points at fifteen seconds is the ceiling.
Rhythm is how you hold attention; the claim count is what you spend it on.

**Close the loop.** Compose the last frame to match the first in framing,
lighting, and product position, so the cut back is invisible. Replays are a
first-class ranking signal and short clips are the easiest place to engineer
one. First-and-last-frame control in current models makes this nearly free.

### Beat sheets

**Five seconds — three changes, one idea, loop-closed.** Word budget 25–40. The
three rows below are the changes, not shots; a single take that hits them works.

| Time | Beat | Shot | Audio | Text |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0–0.4 | hook frame | product or outcome already in frame and moving; no logo, no fade | sound from 0.0, never silence | hook line, ≤5 words, at frame one |
| 0.4–3.0 | single proof | one demonstration of one claim | voice states the claim, or the beat carries it | hook holds or resolves |
| 3.0–5.0 | payoff | hero framing composed to match frame one | the beat lands | offer or call to action |

**Ten seconds — five changes, problem to proof to close.** Word budget 50–90, two
selling points, each with its own visual proof. Add a scale jump and a hand
arrival to the four rows below to reach five.

| Time | Beat | Shot | Text |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0–2.0 | hook | pain state or outcome, in motion | hook text at frame one |
| 2.0–3.0 | product reveal | the product enters, connected to the hook | product name or price |
| 3.0–7.5 | proof | the testable claim, shown; cut around 5.0 | one benefit line |
| 7.5–10.0 | close | hero framing matching frame one | call to action plus offer |

**Fifteen seconds — seven to nine changes, one proof, room for the objection.**
Word budget 75–130, three selling points at most. Write the change column, not a
shot column: the beat is what the viewer notices, and most of them are not cuts.

| t (s) | The change | Kind |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0 | frame one makes the claim, both objects in shot | state |
| 1.5 | hook card, one position | card |
| 3.0 | a hand arrives and takes hold | hand |
| 5.0 | the state flips — closed to open, flat to standing | state |
| 7.0 | scale jump to macro on the thing the claim is about | camera |
| 9.0 | the proof completes: the object lands where it was said to go | object |
| 11.0 | the second card, naming what the frame just showed | card |
| 13.0 | pull back to the full product, loaded | camera |
| 14.5 | the last state, composed to match frame one | state |

The nine rows are a budget, not a template — swap kinds freely, but keep the
gaps at two seconds or under and keep the last row a loop. If the target metric
is saves, row 9 becomes a summary frame holding everything at once; if it is
comments, rows 8 and 9 become the question. `references/viral-anatomy.md` §2.

## Sound

Sound is the intended experience on TikTok — 88% of users call it vital and 93%
spend time with sound on — and a large share of all social video is still
watched muted elsewhere. Build for sound and make the clip legible without it.
[A]

The strongest single audio finding for commerce: **a human voice-over
illustrating the product, combined with a written offer, lifted conversion
87%.** Music appears in 63% of ads, voice-over in 39%. Custom music lifts aided
recall 78%. [A]

**Never name a trending track.** Trending organic audio is generally not
licensed for paid use, and a named track is stale by the time a clip ships.
Prompt for the *structure and the energy* instead: genre, tempo, mood, and where
the cue lands. Tempo convention: 100–140 BPM for most commerce, 120–140 for
fashion and fitness, 80–100 for storytelling and wellness, and the cut should
match the tempo. [C]

**Always name a music bed. It is the switch that turns the audio on.** This
replaces an earlier rule here that said to nominate the loudest foley event; that
was tested on 2026-09-04 and it did nothing.

The measurement, from eighteen generations in one session on one endpoint. Five
segments whose audio line began *"No music at all"* or *"No music, no voice"* came
back at **−47 to −60 LUFS** — silence with dither, cumulative RMS at the −63 dB
noise floor, **no foley whatever**, even though each one named a specific
transient and declared it the loudest event in the clip. Every segment in the
same session that named a music bed came back at **−14 to −17 LUFS**. Rewriting
those five to name a bed lifted them 20 to 35 dB.

So: **there is no such thing as a foley-only clip from this family.** A
foley-forward design still needs a bed underneath it, and the bed is what makes
the foley audible at all.

And **do not qualify the bed's level.** The five rewritten segments named *"a
single sustained low synth pad at a very low level"* and landed at −22 to −40
LUFS — better, still unusable. The segments that landed on target named an
instrument and a tempo and said nothing about volume: *"a warm plucked acoustic
guitar figure at about 105 BPM, no percussion"*. Name the instrument, name the
tempo, say where it enters, and let the mix be the mix.

**Native audio arrives unmastered, and the levels are not postable.** From the
same ten: one family clipped, at -8.0 LUFS with a true peak of +0.9 dBFS on
three of four clips; another sat 3 dB under target; one clip came back at -22.0
LUFS, near inaudible on a phone. Platform target is about **-14 LUFS with a
true peak at or below -1 dBTP**. Put the normalisation step in the pack, because
the seller will not think of it:

```sh
ffmpeg -i in.mp4 -c:v copy -af "loudnorm=I=-14:TP=-1:LRA=11" -c:a aac -b:a 192k out.mp4
```

## On-screen text

Text boxes appear in 86% of ads; the optimal reading speed is **five to ten
words per second**, which lifts awareness 2.1×. [A] That is the budget behind
the beat sheets: two to four short cards for a five-second clip, never a
paragraph.

**Give every card one position, and treat a space inside it as a risk.** A
card written `"这些 全在里面"` came back from a real generation as two cards at
once: the space closed up into 「这些在里面」 at the top of the frame, and
re-opened as a wrapped two-line version at the bottom. A spaced card in the same
run rendered correctly, so this is a risk rather than a rule — one of two spaced
cards split. Where a break is wanted, a punctuation separator carries it
without the ambiguity. Name the position for each card, and put anything that
has to be exactly right in the platform's own text layer.

Keep every critical element inside a conservative safe box — roughly
**x from 110 to 900 and y from 280 to 1230 on a 1080×1920 frame**. That is the
intersection of the published TikTok, Meta, and Shorts safe zones; a
TikTok-only asset can use more of the bottom. Shoppable product cards eat
another 80 to 100 pixels at the bottom. YouTube Shorts generates its thumbnail
from the first frame, so frame one must work as a cover.

Animated captions read as organic; stacked graphic cards read as an ad. In
organic clips captions appear in 80% and standalone text overlays in 4.5%, while
ads use text boxes in 86%. [B] Choose the register deliberately.

**Say aloud** the pain, the promise, the one differentiating claim, and the
offer. **Show, never say** capacity, texture, colour, fit, scale, and speed.
**Put on screen** the price, the call to action, and any number. Two to three
selling points at most, and **每个卖点配一个画面证据** — one visual proof per
selling point. A claim without a proof shot does not convert. [C]

## Writing for AI generation

The audience discount for visible AI-ness is large: 85% say uncanny-valley
elements pull them out of the experience, 29% find AI content obviously fake,
and 49% of US adults would use platforms less if AI content grew. Advertisers
believe young consumers feel positive about AI ads at 82%; only 45% of those
consumers do. [A]

Platforms label automatically through embedded content credentials whether or
not the creator discloses, and undisclosed realistic synthetic media risks
removal or reduced distribution. Never present an AI presenter as a real
customer. [A]

What follows for the prompt: **lean into the shots models render reliably** —
product-only macro, tabletop, texture, staged environment, hands used sparingly
— and away from the shots that trigger detection: talking synthetic faces,
complex hand-and-product interaction, and text rendered on the product. Keep
motion slow and deliberate, and keep each generation under about ten seconds.
[C, consistent with the documented failure modes]

## Category playbooks

### Handbags and totes

Fashion is TikTok's best-converting vertical, and what converts is styling and
try-on rather than a still beauty shot. [A] Lead with **capacity or a
comparison**. The reliable payoff is understated exterior, excessive interior.
The hidden-detail reveal is this category's native unboxing play. Two-bag
comparison is the second-strongest hook shape and prompts as two static
compositions. The persona is the commuter, not the model — which is exactly what
AI renders worst, so favour product-forward shots with an off-camera voice.
Generation risk is medium: hardware, stitching, and logos deform, so work from a
real product photo and keep branding out of frame.

### Lunch bags and insulated bags

The native format is **pack-with-me**: real food, real compartments, real time.
The one provable claim is cold retention, which makes this the best category for
the before-and-after archetype that platforms endorse and almost nobody uses.
Name a third-party object the audience already owns as the fit test — a specific
tumbler, a sixteen-inch laptop — which buys credibility for no words. Two buyers
and two hooks: the parent packing school lunches, and the office worker who is
comparing against buying lunch, which is a loss-aversion hook rather than an
aesthetic one. Answer the bulk objection with a packs-flat shot. Generation risk
is the lowest of the three: tabletop, food, containers, no faces needed.

### Lipstick and lip products

**Get the payoff before second six**: this category's six-second view rate is
about 11%, so nine in ten impressions are already gone. [B] Beauty is TikTok
Shop's largest category by revenue and its ads perform best on tutorial and demo
content. [A/B] Four scenes carry it: the swatch sequence, the application macro,
the half-lip before and after, and the wear or transfer test — each a
single-claim, single-shot beat. The "looks different on everyone" montage is a
documented virality mechanism here. Xiaohongshu wants the opposite clip: a real
tutorial with technique and ingredients, longer and saveable. Generation risk is
the highest of the three, because macro human skin is where anatomical artefacts
show and shade fidelity will not survive text-to-video: mandate image-to-video
from a real product photo, keep the framing tight, keep the face minimal, and
never let the model render the shade name on the tube.

## Seasonal window, September to December 2026

Black Friday falls on **27 November 2026** and Cyber Monday on **30 November**.
TikTok Shop's published category deal days: Home and Electronics 16–18 November,
**Beauty and Lifestyle 19–21**, Wellness 22–24, **Fashion 25–27**, Computers and
Office 1 December. [A]

Posting cadence the platform itself publishes: five to seven shoppable videos a
week in early November, three a day mid-month, five a day over the Black Friday
weekend, then five a week for holiday deal hunters. [A]

By month: September carries the back-to-school tail and fall transition; October
opens gifting research and the Chinese 双11 种草 phase, with Xiaohongshu's 双11
running 11 October to 11 November; November aligns to the deal days and
legitimises price and offer hooks; December turns to last-minute gifting and
recipient-based guides. Black Friday creative is decided in September, because
casting, shipping, and production run in sequence. [C]

## Over-used, and penalised

Hard avoid, because platform policy reaches them:

1. **Comment-bait and like-bait** calls to action. Engagement bait can make
   content ineligible for the For You feed.
2. **Reused or minimally modified content.** A remix workflow must change
   substance, not only the product.
3. **Undisclosed realistic AI.** Labelling happens automatically; design so the
   label does not sink the clip.
4. **An AI presenter implied to be a real customer.** A policy and endorsement
   problem independent of detection.

Soft avoid, because they are crowded or out of favour:

5. **Question openers by default** — the most common hook, sixth of ten on views.
6. **Greeting openers.** There is no setup budget at five to fifteen seconds.
7. **Logo first, slow brand build, static opening frame.**
8. **Polished studio production for cold acquisition.** Native creative earns 74%
   attention and 3.3× the action of non-native formats. [A]
9. **Vague lifestyle framing.** Clarity beats cleverness.
10. **The "That Girl" aesthetic and escapist framings**, which the platform's own
    2026 trend report calls out as fading.
11. **Generic 好物推荐 openers** on Chinese platforms.
12. **Uncanny AI humans.**
13. **Feature lists with no visual proof.**
14. **A named trending track**, which is unlicensed for paid use and stale on
    arrival.
15. **More than one product per clip.**
